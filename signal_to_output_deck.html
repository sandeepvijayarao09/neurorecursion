From motor cortex to cursor: how Neuralink turns spikes into actions

Eight stages take the data from neurons firing in the hand-knob region to clicks, typing and a robot arm. Along the way it shrinks from about 205 Mbps to roughly 1 Mbps, and then to a few intents per second.

Stages 1–2 · Signal source and sensing

The implant listens to the part of the brain that plans hand movement

  • Surgeons find the target before the operation with fMRI while the patient attempts hand movement. It sits on the precentral gyrus.
  • Those neurons still fire when a movement is intended, even with a severed spinal cord.
  • One implant in one hemisphere gives decodable signals for both hands.
  • N1: 1,024 electrodes across 64 flexible threads, each thinner than a hair.
  • The threads are too fine to place by hand, so the R1 robot inserts them. The 2019 robot inserted up to 6 threads a minute with 87.1% success.
  • 2019 threads were polyimide with gold traces, 5–50 µm wide and 4–6 µm thick.
  • Next: about 3,000 electrodes, plus design changes to keep threads in place.
Stage 3 · Analog front end and ADC

Each channel is amplified, filtered and digitised on the implant

Custom low-power chips handle every electrode. The published numbers come from the 2019 research chip, which had 256 channels per chip:

  • The reduction has to run in real time, in under 1 ms, on less than 10 mW.
  • Lossless compression stops around 4–5×, so the implant sends spike events and features, not waveforms.
  • Patent method: filter the signal, fit a model, compare against thresholds, then package each spike with a timestamp.
  • After threads pulled out in 2024, Neuralink retuned this step to pick up broader population activity.
Stage 5 · Wireless link and power

Spike data leaves the skull by radio, with no wires or connectors

  • The processed data goes through the scalp to the Neuralink App on a phone or computer.
  • The battery charges inductively from the N1 Charger. Nothing passes through the skin.
  • The radio is usually described as Bluetooth Low Energy class. Neuralink hasn't confirmed this.
For comparison, the 2019 research rig

was wired: USB-C to a base station that streamed over 10 GbE. Wireless operation is what made at-home, all-day use possible. By Feb 2025 participants averaged 6.5 h a day.

Stage 6 · Decoding, then and now

Pretraining on 50,000 hours of brain data cut calibration time

Before · per-user supervised

  • Each user had a decoder for each feature, trained on calibration tasks.
  • Neural signals drift, so decoders went stale.
  • Users spent about 10 min a day recalibrating, or 55 min a week.

Oct 2026 · pretrained encoder

  • A self-supervised encoder trained on more than 50,000 h of unlabeled data.
  • 30 s of labeled data now matches what 3.5 min of calibration used to give.
  • Decoders last more than a week, and one still worked after 20 months.
  • A model trained on one participant worked for another with 99% of its weights frozen.
Stage 6 · Inside the encoder

Spikes go in as tokens and intents come out of small heads

Mamba backboneState-space model, fully causal and low-latency
→
Decoder headsCursor velocity, click probability, finger/key intent, 7+ arm DOF
Training objective

Spatially masked auto-Poisson regression. The model sees half the channels and predicts spike counts on the other half. No labels are needed, so all of the participants' free-use hours become training data.

Scale

Participant 1 alone contributed more than 9,000 hours, about 22.4 billion spikes. Neuralink is also testing decoding of target position directly, called "cursor teleportation", which exceeded 10 BPS in early tests.

Stages 7–8 · Post-processing and outputs

Decoded intents become ordinary input events

Post-processing
  • Earlier versions used smoothing, low-velocity suppression and click filtering. Each step added lag.
  • Pretrained decoders are stable enough that several of these steps were dropped.
  • A mode switcher maps intents to left, right and middle click, scroll, zoom, pan and drag.
What users do with it
  • Control a computer or phone: chess, Civilization VI, Mario Kart, Fusion 360 CAD, Illustrator.
  • Ten-finger "mind keyboard" typing at up to 40 WPM.
  • Robotic arm control in the CONVOY trial, for feeding and gesturing.
  • In trials or development: speech at about 140 WPM (VOICE) and vision (Blindsight).
